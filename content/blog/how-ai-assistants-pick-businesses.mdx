No AI company publishes its full method, so nobody outside those companies can tell you the exact formula. But we can say what clearly matters in practice: clear facts about your business, trusted sources that mention you, and consistency across the web. This post separates what is reasonably well understood from what is not.

## Do ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity work the same way?

No. They are different products built by different companies. Perplexity describes itself as an answer engine that searches the web and shows its sources. ChatGPT and Gemini can answer from what they learned in training and, depending on the version and the question, can also search the web. Google also adds AI-written summaries to some search results, described in its documentation on [AI features in Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features).

Because the methods differ, the same question can return different businesses in each tool. That is why a fair check asks all three.

## What do assistants use to build an answer?

Two broad sources, in different mixes:

| Source | What it means | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the model learned in training | Patterns from a large amount of text collected up to a past date | Well-covered businesses are easier to recall. New or thin ones may be missing |
| What it finds while answering | Pages found by searching the web at that moment | Pages that answer the question directly are easier to find and quote |

Which source dominates depends on the tool and the question. A question about "the best clinic near me today" is more likely to trigger a web search than a general knowledge question.

## What signals seem to matter?

These are patterns we see, not published rules.

1. **Clear, specific facts.** A page that says what you do, for whom and where is easier to use than a general brochure.
2. **Consistent details.** When your name, address and services match across many places, an assistant can repeat them with confidence.
3. **Independent mentions.** Reviews, directory listings and local news give outside proof beyond your own claims.
4. **Direct answers.** Text that answers a question in its first sentences is easy to quote.
5. **Readable structure.** Clear headings and markup help machines understand a page. Google's [guide to structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/intro-structured-data) explains the basics.

## What do we not know?

Quite a lot, and it is worth being honest about it.

- **The exact weight of each signal.** No one outside the AI companies knows how much a review counts compared with a page you wrote.
- **How often models update.** Training data has a cut-off date, and tools change often.
- **Why a particular answer was chosen.** The same question can give different results from one run to the next.

Anyone who sells a guaranteed method for being named is claiming knowledge nobody has.

## Why do small businesses get missed?

Several ordinary reasons come up again and again:

- **Thin coverage.** The business is mentioned in only one or two places, so there is little to recall.
- **Unreadable content.** Key facts sit in images, PDFs or social posts that machines cannot easily read.
- **Conflicting facts.** Different phone numbers, addresses or names across sites reduce confidence.
- **No specific page.** The site talks about "quality and care" but never says exactly what the business does and where.
- **Newness.** A recent business may not yet appear in the sources a model learned from.

Most of these are fixable. They come down to making your information clearer, more complete and more consistent.

## How should you read the sources an assistant shows?

Some assistants list the pages they used. Treat that list as a free research tool.

1. **Note which sites appear most often.** If the same directory shows up across questions, it is influential in your category.
2. **Open them and look for your business.** Is it there? Is the profile complete and correct?
3. **Look at the winners.** What does the business named first say about itself that you do not?
4. **Check dates.** Old pages may show outdated details that you can ask to have corrected.

The sources change over time, so revisit them every month rather than treating one list as permanent.

A useful habit is to keep a short list of the three to five sources that appear most for your category. For each one, record whether your listing exists, whether the details are right, and who can update it. That list becomes a simple to-do list that you can work through one source at a time.

## Why do answers change between runs?

AI answers are written fresh each time and have some built-in variation. Search results used by the tool can also change from day to day. So the same question asked twice can name different businesses, or the same ones in a different order.

This is why we call a check "a snapshot today". To judge real progress, look at the pattern across many questions and many weeks, not a single result.

## Does ranking on Google decide whether AI names you?

Not directly. A clear, well-built website helps in both places. But a business can rank well on Google and still not be named by an assistant, and the other way round. Assistants also draw on listings, reviews and other sites that never appear on your own page. Our post on [SEO vs AEO](/blog/seo-vs-aeo) explains how the two connect.

## What can a business do with this?

You cannot control the models, but you can control the material they have to work with.

- Keep your facts accurate and identical everywhere.
- Give each service a clear page that names your city and area.
- Be present and complete on the sites that AI tools quote in your category.
- Check regularly, and read the result as a snapshot.
